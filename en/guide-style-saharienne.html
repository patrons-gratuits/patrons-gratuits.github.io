Style guide: safari jacket, safari dress and Bermuda shorts

Colour

Be original: dare to use colour! Safari jackets are traditionally brown or khaki. But thanks to its distinctive features (pockets, epaulettes, sleeve tabs…), your safari jacket will still be a safari jacket, even in colour. You'll simply stand out from the crowd.

Fabric

I cut my safari jackets from the old sheets our grandmothers left in their linen cupboards. They were built to last, in pure cotton, linen-cotton blend or pure linen. They are superb fabrics: they take dye very easily and suit this design particularly well. Linen and linen-cotton also regulate body temperature, so they are very pleasant to wear. Their weave is often more rustic, with coarser threads than today's fabrics, which only adds to the safari look.

Before cutting, unfold the sheet against the light and avoid any worn or thin areas, often in the middle. Then wash it hot: it won't shrink once the garment is sewn.

Buttons

Why settle for ordinary buttons? The military origins of the safari jacket deserve fine gold buttons, plain or patterned: they give it character. Go hunting for them: vintage buttons are often of better quality than most buttons sold today. The ones in the photo below were bought on a second-hand marketplace. They were made by a military button manufacturer: see how they bring out the fabrics.

Buy a complete set: depending on the design and options (flap pockets, epaulettes, sleeve tabs), a safari jacket or safari dress needs 12 to 18 buttons.

For the safari dress

The dress invites the same freedom with colour. A dress and a jacket cut from the same dyed sheet make a lovely outfit. You can also pick two shades from the same family, such as two greens.

For the Bermuda shorts

Choose a fairly heavy sheet, in linen-cotton or heavy linen: shorts work harder than a jacket, in the crotch and at the pockets. The gold buttons go on the waistband and the pocket flaps. The fly buttons stay hidden: simple buttons will do, so keep the fine ones where they show.
